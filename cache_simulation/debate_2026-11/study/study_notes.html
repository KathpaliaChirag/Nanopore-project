How to read this page

Every number carries a label. SIM means simulated cycles from Sniper (never wall-clock seconds of the simulator). REAL means measured on the real Luna machine (2 x Xeon Platinum 8468, shared). UNVERIFIED means I could not confirm it from the repo or it is quoted from a report without re-checking the source.

The one-paragraph version. Sniper first showed a 2x win for a 4-way software k-mer cache on big databases, which then reversed as the workload grew, and hardware cache knobs on their own did nothing. An audit showed the simulator setup could not have answered the question: the 2x was a binary-lineage artifact, and the simulated last-level cache was about 1% of the real one. Real traces on Luna then gave the actual answer: kraken2 does reuse k-mers (81% repeats), but any cache or eviction policy can gain at most about 3% to 5% after the lookup loop is tuned, and a hot-set front cache gains about 0% at 103 GB and loses at 8 GB.

1. The question and the hypothesis

Wrong intuition first. "A cache obviously helps a hash-table workload, so add one and measure." That skips two problems. A software cache costs its own lookup on every query, and the hardware already has three cache levels in front of the table. Whether more cache, different associativity or a different policy helps depends on the access stream, not on the idea.

Hypothesis. A per-thread 4-way set-associative software k-mer cache in front of kraken2's big hash table (the supervisor's design) reduces table probes, and hardware cache knobs (associativity, size, topology) interact with it. The thesis question was "smaller database, smarter cache": which cache organisation is best for kraken2.

Why a simulator. You cannot change the associativity, slice size or replacement policy of the silicon in Luna. A simulator lets you change one knob per run and read hit rates at every level. The cost is that the answer is only as good as the model.

2. Building Sniper on Luna

What we did. Cloned Sniper master (commit 56505e4, 2026-05-23, no release tags exist), installed missing packages, built clean, ran /bin/true in fast-forward and detailed mode. The detailed smoke test gave a believable IPC and a clean memory-model self-check (74 VA-to-PA mappings, 0 violations). Fast-forward IPC (166684) is meaningless, it skips the timing model.

The config pipeline

luna.cfg is an overlay loaded after address_translation_schemes/baseline with two separate -c flags (baseline first). It overrides the cache sizes with the sysfs table above, the clock with 2.1 GHz (the 8468 base clock from Intel's spec, looked up, not measured), and the LLC with a NUCA model: 96 slices of 1125 KB, 15-way. Everything else is inherited: the core model is Sniper's meteor_lake_pcore interval core (no Sapphire Rapids core model ships), DRAM is ddr4_2400, NUCA latencies are placeholders.

The slice size is an approximation twice over. 107520 KB / 96 = 1120 KB, but Sniper aborts unless size = sets x ways x 64 B exactly, and 1120 KB at 15-way needs 1194.67 sets. It was rounded to 1125 KB (1200 sets), a 0.45% error on top of the even-split assumption.

7. The audit: why the simulator results cannot be trusted for Luna

A read-only audit (debate round 1, agent C) recomputed every CSV row. The arithmetic is clean: 189 of 190 rows agree with their IPC column within 0.015 (the one flag is rounding), and cleanstart cycles match simulated time within 0.1%. The numbers are correctly computed. The question is what they measure. Ranked by how much each hides:

  1. The LLC under test was about 1% of the real one. With -n 1 Sniper instantiates one NUCA slice: 1125 KB for "Luna", 2048 KB for the laptop config (confirmed in the effective sim.cfg), against 105 MB real. Only the flat-L3 run saw real capacity, and it was slower (5b). A size or topology idea cannot look positive in a cache that is 1% of the target.
  2. The workload shows almost no reuse inside the window. At 10 to 500 reads, unique cache lines is about the lookup count and LLC hit is 0.2% to 0.5% of loads. This is partly a real property and partly a cold-window artifact (see Chapter 8, where a full trace shows reuse that is long-distance).
  3. DRAM and latency are unvalidated. DRAM is 45 ns, a DDR4 default. Measured Sapphire Rapids DRAM is about 107 ns and L3 about 33 ns (Chips and Cheese, a same-class chip, not Luna UNVERIFIED), so DRAM is about 2.4x too cheap and the 35-cycle NUCA hit about 3x too cheap. The flat-versus-NUCA gap (17% to 31%) is larger than any effect being hunted.
  4. The positive headline was a binary-lineage artifact. S0 and the cache variants are different source snapshots, not one tree with a switch. Evidence below.
  5. The software cache pollutes the hardware cache it was meant to help. At 2000 reads, IPC fell 1.58 to 1.45 and unique lines rose 41%.
  6. Two different cache designs were mixed (original: fixed 4096 sets, static thread-local array, 24 B entries, LRU, raw low-bit set index; current: heap array, 16 B entries, round-robin, murmur index, sizing formula). The weak original index was later shown to give an 8.9x hit-rate change when fixed.
  7. Model artifacts exceed the signal. Instruction-cache modeling was off in every old run (it halves a smoke-test IPC from 0.87 to 0.42), the core is a Meteor Lake model, TLB and prefetch behaviour were never validated.
  8. Instrument limits. Single run per cell, no replicates, uneven read axes, 2 multicore deadlocks, 17 of 27 cleanstart rows.
  9. Config error found 2026-11-23: the LLC slice is half size. luna.cfg divided 107520 KB by 96, but 96 is logical CPUs per socket. lscpu shows 48 cores per socket, so the slice should be about 2240 KB, not 1125 KB. REAL Every Luna-config run used the half-size slice. Signs of conclusions are unchanged (a single slice is about 1% to 2% of the real LLC either way), but absolute LLC-capacity results must not be cited and the config must be fixed before any rerun. The oracle ceiling REAL

A hot-set front table was falsified before it was built. In a copy of the tuned build, for a fixed fraction of keys (chosen by hash bits, so repeats get the same decision, like a hot set) skip both the prefetch and the lookup and reuse the previous taxon. Output is wrong by design, this is a time-only upper bound: a real front table cannot be cheaper because its probe costs time and a hit still needs the value. 32 threads, all 1,872,777 reads, 3 interleaved repetitions. A front table small enough to stay in L2 (262K entries, a few MB) covers 45% to 56% of lookups. At that coverage the oracle gains about 0% at 103 GB and loses about 7% at 8 GB. What it taught
Any idea that changes which lookups reach memory must first pass an oracle ceiling, because the tuned build already overlaps most miss latency and a data-dependent branch in the batch loop is itself costly. Caveats: the trace is 105k reads of one file, so repeat rates are lower bounds for a 1.87M-read run, a per-thread cache at T=32 sees 1/32 of the trace, and the 103 GB profile has few samples.

9. What we can claim, what we cannot, and what to do next

Can claim

  • The method works end to end: Sniper builds on Luna, runs the compiled classify with correct output, and the config pipeline from sysfs is documented bug by bug.
  • Within Sniper with this setup, hardware LLC associativity has no measurable effect (0% to 0.9% spread, 50 MB, 8 GB, 16 GB). SIM Treat this as "this setup cannot see it", not "hardware associativity is irrelevant".
  • Software cache capacity is flat across a 512x range apart from one isolated point at 65536 sets, magnitude +1.0% to +6.3%, cause unknown. SIM
  • On real Luna: 81.4% of lookups repeat, 262K hot slots cover 55.8%, reuse is mostly at distance 2^17 to 2^21, 4-way LRU is within 0 to 6 points of fully associative LRU, and any cache or eviction policy gains at most about 3% to 5% at 8 GB after the lookup loop is tuned. REAL
  • A static hot-set front table is falsified at table sizes that fit in cache: about 0% at 103 GB, a loss at 8 GB. REAL

Cannot claim

  • That a 4-way (or any) software cache is 2x faster on large databases. Lineage artifact.
  • Any simulated speedup in seconds, or any Orion or desktop result as evidence about those chips.
  • That the cache sizing formula is right or wrong for real multi-threaded runs (the sweeps are T=1).
  • That no cache idea could ever help. The bound is for lookups after the loop is tuned, on 8 GB and 16 GB, with a sample of one file. 103 GB lookup share is rough.

Threats to validity

LLC about 1% of real (-n 1 slice). DRAM 45 ns and NUCA latency are placeholders. Core model is Meteor Lake, not Sapphire Rapids. No prefetch or TLB validation. Different binary lineages. Two cache designs mixed. Single runs, no replicates. Tiny cold windows (10 to 500 reads against 1.87M real). No sim-versus-perf calibration anywhere.

Questions a professor might ask

Each answer uses the numbers above. "Trap" is the follow-up that catches people out. Click a question to open it.

Why Sniper and not gem5, ZSim or ChampSim?

The repo records no head-to-head comparison, so the honest answer is practical. Sniper runs real compiled x86 binaries through an Intel SDE trace frontend, exposes every cache level, replacement policy, NUCA topology and DRAM parameter as config keys, and runs at about 200 to 230 KIPS in detailed mode, fast enough for 50 to 100 read windows. We could trace the actual classify binary without writing a model of kraken2.

Claims about gem5 being slower, ChampSim being trace-only for a single core, and ZSim's accuracy are general knowledge, not checked in this repo. UNVERIFIED

Trap: "Did you try another simulator to cross-check?" No. A second simulator would have exposed the DRAM and latency placeholders, so it is a fair criticism.

What does Sniper's interval model do, and how accurate is it?

It does not simulate every pipeline stage. It advances the core in intervals between miss events (cache misses, branch mispredicts) and models overlap of independent misses, memory-level parallelism, as a second-order effect. Newer versions add a ROB-centric core model. The original paper (Carlson, Heirman, Eeckhout, SC 2011) reports about 25% average error against a 16-core Xeon X7460, a figure the debate saw in slides rather than the full text.

Our effects are 1% to 5%, well inside that error band, and our setup was not validated at all.

Trap: "Which core model did you use?" The inherited Meteor Lake P-core interval model with the clock changed to 2.1 GHz. Which variant (interval or ROB) the config selects was never checked. UNVERIFIED

Why cycles and not wall-clock time?

wallclock_s is how long Sniper took, which tracks instruction count and host state. On it, 1-way beat 4-way at 103 GB (346 s versus 360 s) while 4-way was ahead on cycles (47.8 M versus 48.8 M). The same job ran at about 14 s per Mcycle in one batch and about 8 s per Mcycle in a later one, a 1.8x drift from page-cache warmth.

Simulated cycles divided by the clock is the time a real core would take, and every cycle-versus-IPC check held (189 of 190 rows within 0.015). Several older log sentences quote seconds and are now banned.

Trap: "So you never measured real seconds?" Not in Sniper. Real seconds exist only from Luna runs, which are the REAL-labelled numbers.

Why 4-way associativity?

It was the supervisor's baseline design, and the simulated sweeps supported it by a thin margin: best or tied on cycles at all four database sizes (speedup 0.910, 1.830, 2.051, 1.973), 0.7% to 2.1% ahead of 1-way, and 2% to 5% ahead of 16-way on the large databases. On the real trace, 4-way LRU sits within 0 to 6 points of fully associative LRU (38.9% versus 40.1% at 262K).

The simple reasoning: 1-way suffers conflicts, wide ways pay a longer comparison scan, 4-way sits between. The data fit it but do not isolate it.

Trap: "Is 4-way better or just least bad?" Given single runs and a 1% to 2% margin, least bad. And all of it sits on top of a lineage confound.

Which misses dominate here: conflict, capacity or compulsory?

On the real 8 GB trace, compulsory misses (first touches) are 18.6% of lookups, so the hit rate cannot exceed 81.4%. Conflict misses are small: 4-way LRU trails fully associative LRU by 0 points at 16K entries, 1.2 at 262K and 5.5 at 1M. Capacity and policy dominate: at 262K entries LRU gets 40.1% while Belady gets 66.6%.

Because reuse distances are mostly 2^17 to 2^21 slots, a cache needs hundreds of thousands of entries before capacity stops binding.

Trap: "Then why not raise associativity?" It would not help: associativity attacks the smallest of the three gaps.

Why does the cache win vanish as the workload grows?

In the long-read series (same source file, 8 GB) the 4-way / S0 ratio goes 0.704, 0.831, 0.957, 1.031, 1.092 as total bases go 0.35M to 25.4M. Read length is not the driver (10 long reads still win by 30%). The instruction columns show two effects: the two binaries differ by a fixed-size lineage gap that is large relative to small runs, and the cache build executes about 7% more instructions than S0 at 500 reads, the cost of its own lookup code.

So the early "win" is mostly the binary difference being amortised, and the late "loss" is the cache's own overhead. Neither is a hardware-cache effect.

Trap: "So the cache hurts in production?" Not shown. It is neutral on real hardware (S6 ablation, 11.06/14.48 s without versus 11.10/14.55 s with).

Why is hardware LLC associativity flat in the simulation?

Three reasons, all in the data. 99.1% to 99.4% of loads hit L1d and under 0.5% reach the LLC, so the level under test serves about 3 loads per 1000. The simulated slice is 1125 KB, 1.05% of the real 105 MB. And the windows are cold: unique lines is about the lookup count.

The spread is 0.0% to 0.9% with no monotonic trend (500 reads: 4523.2 to 4537.9 M cycles across ways 1 to 30), and with single runs that cannot be classed as noise or effect.

Trap: "Does that prove hardware associativity does not matter on Luna?" No. It proves this setup cannot see it. A real associativity effect on Luna is untested.

What do NUCA and slice sizing mean for -n 1?

The Meteor Lake chain models the LLC as distributed per-core slices on a mesh, not one bank. With -n 1 only one slice is instantiated, so "Luna" had a 1125 KB LLC, the laptop config 2048 KB, the desktop config about 5.3 MB, the Orion config 342 KB. Real Luna has 105 MB.

Only the flat-L3 run (one 107 MB bank) used real capacity, and it came out 17% to 31% slower with identical instructions, because latency does not scale with size in the config and set-count behaviour differs. Multicore runs add one slice per core, which cleanstart fixes by holding total L3 constant.

Trap: "How do you know it was one slice?" The effective sim.cfg in the cleanstart run showed a single 2048 KB NUCA entry with -n 1.

How was the config derived, and how was it validated?

Derived: L1d, L1i, L2 and LLC geometry read from sysfs on Luna, clock from Intel's published base frequency (2.1 GHz), LLC split into per-core slices (1125 KB, 15-way, address hash switched to mod to avoid a crash). Everything else (core model, DRAM, latencies, replacement) is inherited from Sniper defaults.

Validated: only that the caches instantiate with the intended sets and ways, that the run completes, and that classification output matches the native run. There is no timing validation.

Trap: "Is LRU the real LLC policy?" Unknown. It is a placeholder, and real Intel LLC policy is not publicly documented for this generation.

What validation against perf exists?

None. No run was ever compared to perf stat on the same input: not IPC, not LLC misses, not DRAM reads. This is the single largest gap, and the first item in the next-steps list.

The proposed recipe is a random-probe microbenchmark run natively and in Sniper, accepting instructions within 2%, cycles within 15%, LLC misses within 20% and dTLB misses within 25%. That recipe is a proposal from the debate and has not been executed.

Trap: "So the simulated cycle counts mean what?" A consistent ordering within one setup, not a prediction of Luna's time.

What are the main threats to validity?

Ordered by impact: the LLC under test is 1% of real (1125 KB of 105 MB); the headline win is a binary-lineage artifact (instruction gap grows to +32.6 M at 103 GB); DRAM is 45 ns against about 107 ns measured on the same class of chip; NUCA latency is about 3x too cheap; two cache designs are mixed; instruction-cache modeling was off; no calibration; single runs; windows of 10 to 500 reads against 1.87M real.

The CSV arithmetic itself is clean, so the numbers are right and the question they answer is the wrong one.

Trap: "Which one alone would overturn the result?" The lineage confound, since it removes the only positive result.

How did you control for confounds?

What was controlled: wall-clock contamination (switched to cycles, ran the fair batch strictly sequentially), the mislabelled baseline (found the true S0), the IPC consistency of every row, classification output identity (diff against S0 empty), and read length versus total bases (long-read series).

What was not: binary lineage, cache design mixing, LLC size, latency, replicates. The audit found those after the headline had been reported.

Trap: "Why did it take an audit to find the lineage problem?" Because every individual check passed: the arithmetic was right. The confound was visible only by comparing instruction counts across binaries and noticing the gap grows with database size.

Why LRU?

It is Sniper's default and the standard baseline. The real LLC policy on this Xeon is an undocumented adaptive scheme, so LRU is a stated placeholder. For the software cache, the real trace shows policy is not the bottleneck either way: even optimal Belady yields at most about 5% at 8 GB after the loop is tuned.

Hardware replacement policies (SRRIP, SHiP, Hawkeye) were dropped by consensus because they act on levels that see 0.2% to 0.5% of loads.

Trap: "LRU versus Belady is 40% versus 67% at 262K. Isn't that a big gap?" Yes in hit rate, no in time: hit rate x lookup share (7.7%) is 3% versus 5%.

What does the Belady bound mean?

Belady's algorithm evicts the entry whose next use is furthest in the future. It needs the future, so it is offline, and no real policy can beat its hit rate at that capacity. On the real trace it gives 28.0%, 44.1%, 66.6%, 81.4% at 16K, 64K, 262K, 1M entries.

Multiplying by the lookup share of CPU turns it into a time bound: 0.667 x 0.077 = about 5% at 8 GB. It ignores the cost of probing the cache itself, so the real ceiling is lower.

Trap: "Belady at 1M equals 81.4%, why that number?" It equals the repeat rate: with enough capacity and perfect foresight every repeat hits, and only first touches (18.6%) miss.

Why was a hot-set front cache ruled out?

An oracle experiment gave an upper bound before anything was built. Skipping the lookup for a hash-chosen fraction of keys, with wrong output by design, at 45% coverage (about what 262K entries cover) changes 103 GB by -1.3% and 8 GB by +7.1% (slower). Even 60% coverage gives only -5.3% at 103 GB and +5.1% at 8 GB. Only 80% coverage gains 12.5% at 103 GB, and that needs 0.6M to 1M entries.

A real table can only be slower than the oracle, so the idea is falsified at cache-resident sizes. The two-to-four days of building it were saved.

Trap: "The 1M-entry table is untested." Correct, and the oracle says at most about 10% at 103 GB if its probe were free and 0% at 8 GB, so it was not worth building.

What is the contribution or novelty, honestly?

Not a new cache design that wins. The defensible contributions are a measured reuse profile of kraken2 lookups (81.4% repeats, 55.8% covered by 262K slots, reuse mostly at distance 2^17 to 2^21), an optimal-policy bound (3% to 5% at 8 GB after tuning), a priced oracle-ceiling method that kills an idea in a day, and a documented list of Sniper pitfalls for this kind of workload.

Novelty against the literature is not established. The debate reports opened abstracts of TinyLFU, RRIP, ARC, MICA, MemC3, SHARDS and SMARTS (abstract level only, full texts not read). The project notes list kache-hash, MegIS, MetaCache-GPU and GPMeta as must-cite genomics prior art, which I did not open. UNVERIFIED

Trap: "Is there prior art on caching k-mer lookups?" Do not claim there is none. Say a targeted search was not completed.

What would you do with six more months?

First calibrate: a microbenchmark under perf stat and under Sniper with measured DRAM and LLC latency, and a go or no-go on whether Sniper can reproduce real orderings at all. Then single-lineage binaries, full-capacity LLC, sampled windows with confidence intervals and three replicates. Then a Cache Allocation Technology sweep on real Luna (needs root and resctrl, availability unknown) as ground truth for LLC size effects, since the real machine can vary LLC ways and the simulator cannot be trusted to.

Only after that would a new cache or policy idea be worth simulating.

Trap: "Why not just run more reads now?" Detailed mode is about 1 hour per 50 reads and 12 to 15 hours per 2000-read run. Scale needs sampling, not patience.

Why simulate only 10 to 500 reads?

Speed. Detailed Sniper runs at about 200 to 230 KIPS: about 1 hour for 50 reads, 12 to 15 hours per variant for 2000 reads (35.1M bases). Production is 1.87M reads. So every window is a cold start: unique cache lines about equals lookups, and almost nothing is touched twice, which hides exactly the long-distance reuse the real trace later found.

The repair is sampled simulation with functional warming, not longer runs.

Trap: "Does a tiny window bias toward or against caches?" Both ways: no reuse hurts a cache, but a cold fixed startup cost flatters whichever binary has less of it.

Why single runs and no replicates?

Cost and an unexamined assumption that Sniper is deterministic. It is not fully so (the trace reader runs on its own thread), so noise is unbounded. The 0.3% to 0.9% hardware-associativity spreads cannot be classed as noise or effect, and the 1% to 2% margin of 4-way over 1-way is within what an unmeasured noise floor could produce.

The large effects (2x, 30%, a 28% loss at 2000 reads) are far outside any plausible noise. The small ones (width ordering, 65536 spike at +1.0% on 16 GB) are not.

Trap: "Then which conclusions survive?" The ones that rest on large effects or on real-hardware traces.

What are the Orion and desktop configs really?

Cache-size edits on the same Meteor Lake x86 core model, running the same x86 binaries. desktop.cfg uses Ryzen 5 5600X published sizes (L2 512 KB, 32 MB L3 over 6 cores). orion_sizes.cfg uses Jetson AGX Orin sizes (L1 64 KB, L2 256 KB, 4 MB SLC over 12 cores) with unverified associativities.

They test sensitivity of the x86 model to size, not Orion's Cortex-A78AE behaviour. Sniper's x86 frontend cannot trace ARM binaries, so a real Orion run would need DynamoRIO traces recorded on the board.

Trap: "Why is Orion's 8 GB speedup lower?" Unknown. The larger-L1 explanation is untested.

What would falsify your conclusions?

For the real-trace bound: a full-scale run (1.87M reads, T=32) where a per-thread cache's hit rate times lookup share exceeds 5%, or a 103 GB profile with a much larger lookup share than the rough 1.7% of CPU seen. For the oracle: a front table of 600K to 1M entries that stays cache-resident and beats the break-even.

For the simulator audit: a calibration run in which Sniper, after DRAM and LLC fixes, reproduces known real orderings. That would rehabilitate the simulator for new ideas.

Why did the simulator say 8 GB is faster than 50 MB when real Luna disagrees?

The real runs include database load and 32 threads, the simulation is one core and a short window. The 50 MB database also classifies fewer reads (84% versus 98%) and has more DRAM reads (1.11M versus 0.81M). The untested guess is a nearly full table with long probe chains for absent k-mers. Why was the first "2x" result an artifact?

S0 is a different source snapshot from the cache builds. On 8 GB, S0 executes 68.1 M instructions against 51.9 M for 4-way, and the gap grows with database size (+16.2, +25.4, +32.6 M at 8, 16, 103 GB) while it is negative on 50 MB. Why model Sapphire Rapids with a Meteor Lake core?

Sniper ships no Sapphire Rapids (Golden Cove) core model. The baseline address-translation scheme pulls in a Meteor Lake P-core (Redwood Cove) config. Both are wide Intel big-core designs, and L1d and L2 geometry happened to match Luna exactly, so only L1i, the LLC and the clock were overridden.

The ROB size, branch predictor, issue width and latencies are Meteor Lake values. Whether that biases memory-level parallelism, the thing a cache study depends on, was never tested.

Trap: "Is 2.1 GHz right?" It is the documented base clock, not measured, and real runs sit between 0.8 and 3.8 GHz depending on turbo.

How wrong is the DRAM and NUCA latency model?

DRAM in the config is 45 ns, 57.6 GB/s (a DDR4-2400 default). Measured Sapphire Rapids is about 107 ns for DRAM and about 33 ns (125 cycles at 3 GHz) for L3, from a third-party measurement on the same class of chip, not Luna. So DRAM is about 2.4x too cheap and the 35-cycle NUCA hit about 3x too cheap. UNVERIFIED

In the cleanstart runs about 0.8M to 1.1M DRAM reads occur per 50 reads in 373M to 446M cycles, so DRAM is a minority of cycles and an error of that size moves the totals by tens of percent, enough to swamp 1% to 5% effects.

Trap: "Which direction does the error bias?" Depends on the experiment: size sweeps get biased toward bigger, cache-avoids-LLC-trip tests toward worse. The sign cannot be trusted.

What is the 65536 anomaly and how big is it really?

Across the six-size ladder, cycles are flat except at exactly 65536 sets: +6.3% (50 MB), +2.5% (8 GB), +1.0% (16 GB), +6.0% (103 GB). The first write-up cited +4.4% and +10.2% for 8 GB and 103 GB, but those were instruction deltas, an error caught by cross-checking the CSV.

A linear initialisation-cost theory was overturned when cycles reverted at 262144 and above. The leading guess, a hash-distribution artifact at that mask width, is untested, as is a build-quirk check of that one binary.

Trap: "Is it a real effect or noise?" It recurs at the same set count on four databases, so not random noise. But the workloads differ by panel (10 versus 50 reads) and the cause is unknown.

Why was flat L3 slower than NUCA?

Instructions are identical (35.8 M and 68.1 M) and L3 hit is 0.02% and 0.11%, so caching behaviour did not cause the +30.7% (50 MB) and +16.6% (8 GB) cycle difference. The same 15 + 20 cycle latency placeholder was used for a 107 MB bank as for a 1.1 MB slice, and the set counts differ (114,688 versus 1,200), which changes mapping and queuing behaviour in the model.

The point for the talk: the topology assumption moved cycles by 17% to 31%, far more than any associativity knob (under 1%), so the model's structure outweighs the signal.

Trap: "Which one is right?" Neither is validated. NUCA is the architecturally realistic one.

What did cleanstart actually establish?

A trustworthy-in-method 1-core baseline (9 of 9 cells, S0, laptop config with instruction-cache modeling and Zen 3 TLBs on) and a 4-core set with 8 of 9 cells, and an infrastructure with a hang watchdog and one-knob-per-run naming. 1-core cycles range from 80.3 M to 1096.0 M, L1d hit 99.1% to 99.4%.

It established no hardware-knob effect, since none of the L3, L2, L1 or associativity sweeps was run. The 8-core group has no rows, and the 1-core L3 size needs re-checking in the effective config.

Trap: "What is the one-line result?" A baseline showing the hierarchy beyond L1 serves under 1% of loads.

What were the multicore deadlocks and how were they handled?

Two of 14 four-core launches froze at startup: all four processes sleeping at 0% CPU, log ending at "Thread N started", no sim.stats. One ran 9 hours before being noticed. Zero of 9 single-core runs hung, and a 2-read 4-core smoke test passed, so a startup race is suspected but not shown.

A watchdog now kills a run whose session CPU grows less than 60 CPU-seconds in 900 s and writes a HUNG row so the queue continues. The first rule (CPU time unchanged) never fired, because the Python wrapper keeps accruing a little CPU.

Trap: "Could hangs bias the results?" The hung cells are missing, not wrong, but missing cells can still bias a sweep.

"No reuse" was the old story. What changed?

The 10 to 500 read windows showed unique lines about equal to lookups and LLC hits under 0.5%, so "no reuse" was inferred. The real trace of 12.2M lookups shows 81.4% repeats and 90% of lookups covered by 1M slots. Reuse exists but at distance 2^17 to 2^21 slots (about 77% of repeats), far beyond L1 or L2 and beyond the cold window.

It also matches an earlier real reuse-distance histogram in the project (54% repeats at T=1 on 3.0M lookups, quoted from the round 2 report, not re-opened), so the correction is not a one-off. UNVERIFIED

Trap: "So a big cache does help?" Hit rate yes (76.5% at 1M entries), time no: lookups are about 7.7% of CPU, so the cap is single digits.

Why does a front cache lose at 8 GB in the oracle?

The oracle's skip decision is data-dependent and near 50/50 at 45% coverage, so the branch mispredicts often, and skipped keys leave holes in the 16-lookup prefetch batches. At 8 GB the table is partly cache-resident and prefetch already hides most latency, so there is little time to save and the branch cost dominates: +7.1% at 45%.

That is the most likely explanation, not a measured one. At 103 GB, where lookups are more latency-bound, the same skip gives -1.3% at 45% and -12.5% at 80%.

Trap: "A real front cache has no mispredict if branchless." Possible, but a real probe also has its own latency, and the oracle charges none.

How were the 4-way LRU and Belady hit rates computed?

Offline, by replaying the traced slot-index sequence (12,237,762 lookups) through each policy: fully associative LRU, 4-way set-associative LRU, and Belady's optimal. Code is debate_2026-11/e1/analyze.cpp, raw output analysis_8gb_T1.txt. The trace is the table-slot sequence, so a hit means "this slot was looked up recently enough to be in an N-entry structure".

Caveats: one thread (a T=32 per-thread cache sees about 1/32 of the stream), 105k reads of one file (repeat rates are lower bounds for 1.87M reads), and the cache's own probe cost is excluded.

Trap: "Is this the same as measuring a cache in the program?" No, it is a bound on hits, not time.

Is the lookup share (7.7%) reliable?

For 8 GB and 16 GB, reasonably (perf record on 105k reads, one thread, 7.7% and 6.9% of CPU, about 15% of user-space time). For 103 GB it is rough: 1.7% of CPU, about 28% of user time, with only about 450 user samples because the run is dominated by database load at one thread. Kernel database-load time is 20% to 65% of samples depending on database.

Because the 3% to 5% bound uses 8 GB, 103 GB may allow more. That is why the next honest step is a 32-thread, 1.87M-read profile on 103 GB.

Trap: "Why does the oracle show 12.5% at 103 GB then?" At 80% skip it removes work that is a larger fraction there. It does not contradict the bound, which was for 8 GB.

What exactly do you claim for the thesis?

A negative-result thesis with positive method: kraken2 lookups have heavy, long-distance reuse; hit-rate-optimal caching and eviction cannot give more than about 3% to 5% at 8 GB after the lookup loop is tuned; a static hot-set front cache is ruled out by an oracle ceiling; and the earlier simulated 2x was an artifact of binary lineage and a 1% LLC. The simulator itself needs calibration before it is used for new designs.

I would not claim a new cache that speeds up kraken2.

Trap: "Isn't a negative result unpublishable?" A priced upper bound with a reproducible oracle method is publishable as characterisation, if the claims stay within the bound.

Is the 105 MB LLC and the 96 slices consistent?

Trap: the large compact_stall and compact_fail counters look alarming, but they count background compaction attempts, not fallbacks to 4 KB pages that the process experienced.

Why did the same prefetch depth that did nothing at 32 threads help at 96 threads?

The number of misses in flight scales with threads times batch size. At 32 threads a batch of 16 already kept the memory system busy enough, so going to 64 was a null. At 96 to 128 threads on the 103 GB table, a batch of 64 gained 3 to 7% (103 GB: 7.08 to 6.88 s; optimum 64 to 128, flat to 256). The machine is not bandwidth bound: 66M lookups per second is about 4.2 GB/s, 1.4% of DRAM peak. So the limit is latency and queueing in the memory system, not bandwidth. REAL

Trap: a tuning constant found at one thread count does not transfer; a sweep has to be redone after any change that raises concurrency.

Did you try different prefetch hints, and why do they not matter?

Yes: prefetcht0 (all levels), t1, t2 and prefetchnta, selectable at run time, 5 interleaved repetitions at 96 threads on three databases. All four are within about 2% (103 GB medians 6.51, 6.66, 6.67, 6.53 s; 8 GB and 16 GB within 0.02 s). The lines are touched once, so the cache level they land in does not change how many misses occur. REAL

Trap: "non-temporal should help for one-shot lines": it showed no gain, and it can hurt by evicting hot lines from the shared 105 MB L3.

Is loading the database bandwidth-bound?

Yes. Loading the 104 GB table into huge-page memory ran at 35 to 40 GB/s (2.8 to 3.1 s) for every method tried: pread, mmap plus memcpy, MADV_POPULATE_READ, chunk sizes from 16 to 256 MB, and 32 or 96 threads. Splitting the page zeroing from the copy (MADV_POPULATE_WRITE first) was slower, 4.0 to 4.8 s. At 38 GB/s the copy moves about 100 GB/s of total memory traffic (read the page cache, zero the huge page, write the copy). Only skipping the copy removes the cost: a resident database, or -M, which trades load time for 4 KB-page TLB misses (+4.9 s net). REAL

Trap: "use more loader threads": 32 to 96 threads changed the time by 6%.

Why is the 103 GB case so different from the 8 GB case?

On 8 GB the run is compute bound: perf stat shows IPC 2.31, with the scanner and classification loop dominating. On 103 GB the table does not fit in any cache, so GetAt stalls on DRAM (29.5% of samples at 96 threads), IPC drops to 1.59, and 3.2 s of the run is the table load. The same code, a different bottleneck. REAL

Trap: a profile at one database size (or at one thread count) does not describe the others: the lookup share of CPU was 7.7% at 8 GB with one thread and 16 to 19% at 32 threads.

What does the real-machine evidence say about the earlier Sniper study?

The bottlenecks we found on the real machine are queueing and bandwidth effects (prefetch depth versus thread count, load traffic, lock contention in the reader, branch mispredictions at 1.3 per base). Sniper as configured had a fixed 45 ns DRAM, no prefetch or TLB model, no thread contention, a half-sized LLC slice and 10 to 500 read windows. A model that could rank these designs would need a calibrated memory-level-parallelism model, which was never built. The cheaper tool that worked was the time-only oracle ceiling: skip a fraction of the lookups on the real machine and measure the bound. REAL

Trap: do not present the Sniper numbers as evidence about Luna; present them as a simulator-only exploration with known modelling gaps.
